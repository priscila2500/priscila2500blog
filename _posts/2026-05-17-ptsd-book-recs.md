These are the books I’ve read on CPTSD (complex post-traumatic stress disorder), I’ll rank them from personally most helpful to helpful. This list of books primarily targets patients, not clinicians.

I started my healing journey around 2021.

**Complex PTSD: From Surviving to Thriving by Pete Walker**

This book is probably the most helpful to someone dealing with active symptoms, such as CPTSD flashbacks. Pete Walker is a psychotherapist in the San Francisco Bay Area, and he had 20 years of experience working with CPTSD patients when he wrote this book in 2017. He is a very empathetic writer, and his descriptions of what CPTSD patients emotionally deal with are very concrete and anchored in patients’ poignant descriptions. As an example, when trying to explain how vulnerable children are during episodes of emotional neglect, he describes a patient recalling that as a small child, such was the abandonment they felt they had wanted to throw themselves into traffic out of desperation. The book has many patient recollections such as this one. Pete Walker treats CPTSD seriously, and after reading his book, many people have a better understanding of what it is and whether it applies to them or not. The book has plenty of references; some concepts he describes, such as the Fight, Flight, Fawn, and Freeze responses to trauma, are ideas from other writers in the field. Interestingly, he introduces some of his own ideas that I and many others have found helpful, such as abandonment mélange, or what he aptly describes as the outer critic. He has a chapter on managing flashbacks that is worth gold. I have this book in print and in audiobook, and I have perused it extensively in the first couple of years of my healing journey.

**Journey Through Trauma by Gretchen Schmelzer**

Gretchen Schemelzer is a psychologist, and she had about 25 years of experience when she wrote this book in 2018. I get the sense that she could have written a book for clinicians, but wrote a book for patients instead. The reason she wrote this book was that she often witnessed her patients abandoning treatment because their healing work is often painful and vulnerable, and unfortunately, it is the case that patients feel as if they are getting much worse before getting better. She wrote a book that offers a close portrayal of what healing from trauma actually looks like, because it isn’t what we would expect. It is a journey, and improvement is not linear; it’s closer to cyclical. The phases the book describes are preparation, unintegration, identification, integration, and consolidation. In the preparation phase, the patient reaches safety, something that is necessary before beginning the work in the next stages. In the unintegration phase, the stress-loaded psyche feels safe enough to come apart a bit, and it does so by resurfacing grief and old material. When this happens, patients recoil because it is painful and unexpected, often coming years or decades after survival, and patients feel done and want to abandon treatment. I found this book genuinely incredible at the time I read it, not only that someone had gone through the journey themselves, but that they had described it in such detail. I find it helpful to read the book and identify what stage one is working through. As a note, Schmelzer’s writing style is a tad literary; she uses plenty of metaphors and other narrative devices she finds helpful. I like her writing style, but can see why others might not. I keep this book as a reference and continue to use it.

**What My Bones Know by Stephanie Foo**

Stephanie Foo is an Asian American, a radio journalist, producer, and author. She used to work on This American Life radio show when during her 30s, after achieving some stability and career success, she experienced a physical and mental breakdown without apparent reason that made her cry for days on end and rendered her unable to work. She went on a career leave and used all her radio journaling skills to investigate what was happening to her and the root cause of it. The journey of what she learned about herself, her past, and her CPTSD diagnosis resulted in this book. The book is part autobiographical and part investigative non-fiction, both aspects woven skillfully together. Stephanie Foo's book is an important contribution, as she is Asian American, a community that has particular challenges around the discussion of mental health concerns. This book is a friendly, first-time introduction to CPTSD, and it's the book I would gift to someone else to introduce them to the topic. The resource section at the end of the book is gold.

**The Body Keeps the Score by Bessel van der Kolk**

Bessel van der Kolk is a very influential psychiatrist, researcher, and educator in the trauma-informed psychiatry space. He worked with Vietnam War veterans dealing with PTSD around 1978 at the Boston Veterans Administration Outpatient Clinic and in 1982 moved to Massachusetts Mental Health Center to teach psychopharmacology. His experience with combat veterans made him sensitive to their ailments, this made him notice in turn that patients presenting at the clinic shared similar symptoms (flashbacks, self-harm behaviours, episodes of rage, relationship problems etc). During his career he has done a lot of research that helped shift trauma being understood as a psychological memory problem into something that affects a whole individual’s organism: body, nervous system, attachment, attention, emotional regulation, identity, development etc. He’s a great educator and writer and makes wonderful metaphors that have helped his ideas spread. He released the book The Body Keeps the Score in 2014.

This book is replete with information on research he’s done regarding how trauma affects the brain and how different therapies can help patients. It helped me because I found that the science validated that what I was dealing with was real and not, as my brain was telling me at times, something that I was making up to excuse my failings. The book has some critics that point out that the book goes into pseudoscience, especially in the section regarding therapies, and I agree that van der Kolk can seem overly enthusiastic in describing the promise of experimental therapies, but hey, this man is a giant in the field and there has been doubt about his intuitions since the 80s. This book is more informative than therapeutic, and it’s a great introduction to the topic for a general audience.

**Trauma and Recovery by Judith Herman**

Judith Herman is a psychiatrist, researcher, and teacher, and she introduced the concept of “complex trauma” in this book from 1992. She made the observation that individuals exposed to repeated relational trauma had symptoms beyond the traditional PTSD diagnosis.  Before trauma and therapy speak became more common in the late 2010s, popular culture conceived of mental health issues as self-inflicted individual problems due to poor character or lack of willpower. This book was groundbreaking as Herman outlined the connection between social justice, trauma, mental health, and relationship issues. The book has two parts: part 1 discusses trauma, and part 2 discusses recovery. The phases of recovery she identifies are safety, remembrance and mourning and finally, reconnection.

 I would not recommend this book as a first introduction to CPTSD as it is a difficult read for a survivor's first time reading, especially not the first part of the book as it goes into detail regarding political terror, war crimes and other such horrors. Herman’s voice carries through the book with strength, and you can get a sense of the urgency of her message in her writing. I understand this book to be an argument to other clinicians and to the medical establishment to reframe their work, I don't think this is a book meant for patients.
